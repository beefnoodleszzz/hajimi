# Fact pack — cloud weight

## Verified facts

1. Clouds have weight because they contain water. The exact estimate depends on whether one counts only water droplets or the droplets plus the air inside the cloud.
2. The U.S. Geological Survey gives an example using a cumulus cloud with a volume of one cubic kilometre and an estimated liquid-water density of 0.5 grams per cubic metre.
3. That calculation gives about 500,000,000 grams, or about 500,000 kilograms: approximately 1.1 million pounds, or about 551 tons.
4. The same USGS explanation says a cloud can float because the cloudy, moist air is less dense than the drier air around it.
5. When water droplets become large enough that the upward motion can no longer keep them suspended, they fall as rain.

## Safe wording

- Say “a one-kilometre-cube cumulus estimate is about 551 tons,” not “every cloud weighs 551 tons.”
- Say “liquid water droplets,” not “the cloud is a solid block of water.”
- Say “less dense cloudy air and upward motion help support it,” not “gravity stops acting on the cloud.”

## Source

- U.S. Geological Survey, “How Much Does a Cloud Weigh?” https://www.usgs.gov/water-science-school/science/how-much-does-a-cloud-weigh

## Editorial note

The density comparison and the “water is not weightless” line are explanatory framing. The numerical estimate and buoyancy explanation are attributed to the USGS source above.
